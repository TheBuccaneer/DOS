                                                                                           Abstract
                                                   LLM inference is inherently expensive, even a modest slowdown can translate into
                                                   substantial operating costs and severe availability risks. Recently, a growing body
                                                   of research known as latency attacks focuses on crafting inputs to trigger worst-case
                                                   output lengths. However, we report a contrary finding that these algorithmic-level
                                                   latency attacks are largely ineffective against modern LLM serving systems. We
                                                   reveal that system-level optimization such as continuous batching provides a logical
                                                   isolation to mitigate contagious latency impact on co-located users. Thus, in this
                                                   paper, we shift our focus from the algorithm to the system layer, and introduce a
                                                   new Fill and Squeeze attack strategy targeting the state transition of the scheduler.
                                                   “Fill” first exhausts the global KV cache to induce Head-of-Line blocking, while
                                                   “Squeeze” forces the system into repetitive preemption. By manipulating output
                                                   lengths using different attack prompts, and leveraging side-channel probing of
                                                   memory status, we demonstrate that the attack can succeed in a practical black-
                                                   box setting with much less cost. Extensive evaluations on vLLM indicate up to
                                                   75 − 742× TTFT degradation relative to benign baselines and 1.5 − 4× average
                                                   slowdown on Time Per Output Token compared to existing attacks with 30-40%
                                                   lower attack cost. Code: https://github.com/Phil-Fan/FS-attack


                                        1     Introduction
                                        LLM API spending has surged from $3.5B to $8.4B in the last six months [23], driven by billion-
                                        scale daily requests [34]. Meeting strict latency requirements of conversational agents [28, 3], code
                                        generation [39, 4], and document summarization [44, 54] while maximizing GPU utilization is the
                                        core challenge of modern LLM serving. As AI companies are competing to accommodate larger
                                        context windows (e.g., 1M/65K input/output tokens for Gemini 3.0 Pro [13]), a new class of Denial-
                                        of-Service threats is looming known as latency attacks [8, 19, 32, 11, 5, 20, 56, 59] that exploits
                                        the autoregressive nature of LLMs to suppress the end-of-sequence (EOS) token and trap models
                                        in indefinite babbling. Existing literature reports 7 − 10× latency amplification [11] that could
                                        theoretically threaten service availability.
                                        However, counter-intuitively, we first find that the existing model-centric latency attacks are surpris-
                                        ingly ineffective in production environments. Through extensive empirical and theoretical analysis,
                                        we find that simply prolonging generation length fails to inflict significant latency on co-located
                                        users, and often leaves Time-to-First-Token (TTFT) nearly unchanged even when malicious requests
                                        constitute a large fraction of the workload. To answer “Why latency attacks fail to delay?”, we
                                        delve into the natural resilience of Continuous Batching (CB) [18, 52, 57, 21], a technique originally
                                        proposed to schedule requests at the iteration level so that short requests do not need to wait for
                                        the long requests to finish. This helps create a logical isolation between the normal traffic and the
                                        attacker’s long-running request. Even if the attacker could occupy the scheduling slot, it does not
                                        prevent shorter, legitimate requests from entering and exiting the system. As a result, this relegates
                                        most latency attack as largely self-inflicted rather than contagious on co-located users [50].

To make latency attacks effective again, we shift our focus to the system layer by introducing
Fill and Squeeze (F&S), a new attack strategy targeting the deterministic state transitions of the
scheduler. It can be integrated with any attack methods as pluggable payloads. The core intuition
is to manipulate memory resources to trigger pathological behaviors rather than simply slow down
individual generations. The proposed attack operates via two vectors: 1) Fill, which focuses on
exhausting the VRAM rapidly. By injecting adversarial requests that generate long sequences and
potentially leveraging ambient traffic peaks [41], the attacker could quickly drive the global KV cache
usage to a boundary point that triggers the scheduler’s admission control, inducing memory-based
Head-of-Line (HOL) blocking; 2) Squeeze, which exploits the preemption logic. Once the system is
brought to the saturation boundary, it forces the scheduler into an alternation between continuous
preemption and recovery, that diverts precious GPU cycles into expensive memory swapping or
recomputation operations [42, 49].
Yet, implementing this strategy in a production environment faces two challenges. The first one
is the visibility gap. Different from white-box KV cache estimation [22], the attacker acts as a
standard tenant with no privileged access to system internals (current KV cache occupancy). Hence,
it is difficult to determine the exact timing to launch an injection and whether the injection has
brought the system to the boundary proximity of GPU VRAM. Second is the economic viability. A
brute-force strategy of continuously sending maximum-length requests incurs high monetary costs
and low stealthiness [2, 26]. In the real world, where attackers must pay per token, an attack is
only worthwhile if the damage outweighs the cost. Hence, it is necessary to come up with a more
cost-effective strategy that can take advantage of the deterministic states of the serving scheduler.
To tackle these challenges, we first discover that the Inter-Token Latency (ITL) of standard requests
exhibits a strong correlation with global GPU memory usage. We leverage this proxy to train a
lightweight regressor [17] that allows the attacker to estimate KV usage without system privileges in
black-box. Further, to minimize attack cost, we formulate the attack into a constrained optimization
problem, decompose attack prompts into three different tiers measured by output lengths and design a
dynamic injection strategy to switch between the Fill and Squeeze at different intensities. The results
show 20 − 280× average slowdown on Time to First Token (TTFT) and 1.5 − 4× average slowdown
on Time Per Output Token (TPOT) compared to the existing attacks.

2     Background and Related Works
2.1   LLM Serving

LLMs generate text autoregressively via prefill and decoding, in which decoding continues until an
end-of-sequence [EOS] token is produced or a predefined maximum length is reached. Because each
decode step must read the accumulated KV-cache, it is memory-bound. Production serving systems
like vLLM [18], Orca [52], SGLang [57] maximize throughput via Continuous Batching (CB), which
schedules requests at the iteration level [52, 31]. When any running request completes, a new waiting
request is immediately admitted, preventing short requests from queuing behind long ones. When
aggregated demand exceeds capacity, the scheduler either preempts active requests (via swap to CPU
DRAM or KV recomputation) or blocks new admissions.
To handle real-world dynamic request lengths, Sarathi-Serve [1] proposes chunked prefill to split long
prompts into smaller batches, piggybacking them onto decoding steps to reduce pipeline bubbles.
DynaServe [27] introduces a unified execution plan that dynamically partitions resources between
prefill and decode to handle unbalanced workloads. Similarly, Aqua decouples memory and compute
allocation via network-accelerated memory offloading [41]. To address user fairness, VTC employs
fair queuing to ensure equal resource distribution [31]. Importantly, there is a distinction between
handling natural long-context traffic and malicious latency attacks. In production environments,
the arrival of long requests follows a stochastic distribution (e.g., Poisson process) [31, 18, 27, 36].
Modern schedulers are robust to handle occasional congestion by swapping them to DRAM or
delaying their admission until resources free up. In contrast, our work approaches LLM serving from
an adversarial perspective by transforming the VRAM capacity constraint into a failure mode that
standard scheduling policies (like FCFS or LIFO preemption) cannot easily resolve.

2.2   Latency Attacks

Latency attacks on AI systems evolve under the classic DoS principle to induce computational
overload. The pioneering examples are the series works of Sponge Examples [32, 5, 30], which


                                                   2
targets NLP/vision models to deliberately inflate inference time and energy usage. Latency attacks
can be broadly categorized into: optimization-based, whose objective is to postpone the generation
of EOS token for as long as possible [8, 19, 10, 33]; prompt engineering-based, which exploits the
model’s instruction-following capability to maximize the output length [20, 56, 59]. It is achieved by
constructing prompts that involve complex logical reasoning or force the model to explain multiple
concepts. Although the existing attacks are theoretically sound, we demonstrate that most of these
model-centric approaches struggle in production environments. In contrast, this work targets the
scheduler by exploiting the resource management logic rather than optimizing the generation length.

The attack operates in four phases: ➊ payload-agnostic prompt construction, ➋ black-box KV state
probing, ➌ cost-optimal injection, and ➍ adaptive feedback control. These phases jointly enable the
attacker to estimate the current KV-cache state, select the cheapest prompt combinations that can
cross the saturation boundary, and avoid self-preemption while sustaining disruption.
➊ Payload-Agnostic Prompt Construction. A primary objective of the attacker is to utilize tiered
prompts to save attack cost and avoid detection, i.e., P = {PHigh , PMid , PLow } and maximized
Expansion Ratio Lout /Lin . PHigh fills the KV cache for rapid depletion free_block_queue and
PLow tips an already saturated scheduler into preemption, which is also used for probing. PMid
sustains memory pressure during transitional states. Our method supports any pluggable payload
(different latency attacks) that can induce long outputs. In this work, we instantiate it with plain-text
prompts for black-box transferability [25, 9] and ExtendAttack [59] to trigger extended reasoning.
➋ Black-Box KV-Cache Estimation via ITL Probing. A practical challenge of launching F&S
attack is to know the system KV-cache utilization in real-time. Prior work such as Helix [22] assumes
privileged access to request output lengths and historical statistics. However, in black-box adversarial
settings, the attacker is also an external tenant and lacks direct visibility into the global scheduler
state. Let Usys denote the fraction of used KV-cache blocks over total blocks. Based on Finding #2
and Proposition 2, we could infer Usys through the ITL-based side channel induced. Operationally,
the attacker periodically dispatches probe requests pprobe ∈ PLow and records their ITL sequence
t = (t1 , t2 , . . . , tk ) for estimation.
It turns out that the attacker can use a lightweight classifier, Mθ : Rk → [0, 1] to map the ITL
sequence to discrete KV-usage bins with high accuracy in real-time. For instance, we can instantiate
Mθ with LightGBM [17]. Compared with sequence models, LightGBM achieves higher accuracy
and substantially lower inference latency, which allows the attacker to synchronize injection timing
with the scheduler state at sub-second granularity. As shown in Fig. 4c, the predicted KV usage
consistently tracks the ground truth across both gradual accumulation and abrupt memory release.
The low variance in Fig. 4b further indicates that the probe is robust to stochastic scheduling noise.

5     Experiments
5.1   Threat Model

We consider an LLM inference service using default vLLM v0.11.2 [18]: a shared global KV pool,
FCFS admission (no skip-ahead), LIFO preemption, no per-tenant KV quota, and client-visible
streaming timestamps, with no input filtering applied [16, 55, 2]. The attacker is a black-box tenant
with only public API access and no knowledge of model parameters or runtime internals; in our
usage “black-box” means no victim-side access, not that a single frozen estimator transfers across
hardware, and the ITL probe of Sec. 4 is trained on a matching surrogate. Using standard concurrency,
the attacker synchronizes long-context queries; when rate limits are enforced per account, multiple
identities keep aggregate pressure up, and this still does not bypass entity-, payment-, or global-level
enforcement. Unlike prior self-inflicted latency attacks [8, 19, 59, 56], our goal is to degrade service
for co-located benign users through hardware contention that continuous batching cannot isolate. F&S
targets the setting above and is not a claim of universal bypass: strict entity-level quotas, skip-ahead
admission, fair queuing, output buffering, and hard length caps each weaken it in a different way
(Sec. 6).

5.2   Experimental Setup

Serving Environment. We conduct experiments on a server equipped with 8× NVIDIA L40S GPUs
(48GB VRAM) powered by vLLM v0.11.2 [18]. We use mainstream open-source model families
including Qwen3-8B [51], Gemma3-12B-it [38] and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B [14], and set
the gpu_memory_utilization to 0.90 to allocate 90% of the VRAM for KV-Cache and model
weights. This setup represents a standard production node and the analysis can be easily extended to
large-scale GPU clusters. More setup details are available in Appendix C.
Workload and Datasets. Following [31, 18, 27, 36], we first emulate normal traffic with Poisson
distribution for the main results (Sec. 5.3) and adopt Alpaca [37] as the primary dataset, since its
consistent instruction-following format provides a stable baseline for evaluating core performance
metrics. Additionally, we also utilize ShareGPT dataset [6] for ablation studies.
Baselines. We benchmark our attack against Engorgio [8], LoopLLM [19] and ExtendAttack [59].
These baselines primarily function as algorithmic complexity attacks that are oblivious of the sched-
uler’s state. For F&S strategy, we utilize plain-text prompts and ExtendAttack as the underlying
methods for cross-model transferability and maximum attack strength, respectively. For fair compari-
son, we deploy the baselines using a fixed-interval injection. In each slot, the adversarial prompt is
randomly sampled from the adversarial prompt collections. This represents the baseline attackers
that are not aware of the system state and relevant cost. Note that our study is based on the existing
fixed-schedule attacks, rather than adaptive wrappers on the existing schemes. The component
ablation in Appendix D.1 further demonstrates that merely sending adaptively is still insufficient.
Evaluation Metrics. Attack effectiveness is evaluated via the following metrics. For user-perceived
latency, we first examine TTFT. This metric represents the initial wait time and an extended TTFT
implies failure to acknowledge user inputs due to queue congestion. Second, we monitor TPOT to
assess generation quality. Its degradation represents non-smooth interaction that causes the model to
get stuck or hang on generating subsequent tokens. In addition, we also measure the 99th percentile
(P99) latency for both TTFT and TPOT. It captures the tail-end user experience, representing the
worst-case delay felt by the vast majority of users. To measure the system burden, we track the
number of preemption (cumulative _preempt() invocations), which quantifies the frequency of
scheduler thrashing and indicates additional overhead of recomputation or memory swap. All main

5.3     Main Results

First, we analyze the attack effectiveness on a single GPU and scale up to multiple GPUs later. Table 1
demonstrates that our strategy achieves a superior balance between attack efficacy and economic
cost compared to the baselines. We can see that the existing scheduler-oblivious attacks rarely drive
the system into HOL blocking. In contrast, our state-aware strategy consistently pushes the system
to the saturation boundary by maximizing HOL events, yielding catastrophic TTFT inflation. E.g.,
when paired with plain-text prompts (F&S+plain-text), we achieve a significant 75× TTFT slowdown
on Qwen3-8B (0.15 s → 11.05 s), and 742× (0.13 s → 97.83 s) on Gemma3-12B-it. Meanwhile,
F&S+ExtendAttack magnifies degradation even further. E.g., on Qwen3-8B, TTFT further increases
to 35.95 s. Both methods carry much lower cost compared to the baselines.

Multi-GPU Tensor Parallelism. Intuitively, a natural defense against resource exhaustion is over-

High-Intensity prompts (PHigh ) without inducing thrashing. TP8 provides ample resources that
allow benign users to aggressively occupy scheduling slots, effectively starving the attacker of
admission and neutralizing the threat at a lower total cost. Other vLLM parameters primarily shift
the attack cost profile rather than its feasibility. E.g., block_size controls allocation granularity
near saturation; preemption mode (recomputation vs. swap) and swap_space determine the per-
preemption recovery cost, and the scheduling policy dictates whether FCFS-HOL exists. A detailed

6   Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we show that existing model-centric latency attacks only impose minor performance
impact on modern serving systems, because continuous batching isolates co-located requests at the
iteration level. We therefore target the scheduler instead. By exploiting the ITL side channel and
tiered prompts, F&S pushes the shared KV cache into saturation and repeated preemption, and inflicts
much higher latency on co-located users at lower attack cost.
On the other hand, simple defense such as per-account rate or concurrency limits only slow one
Fill instance when requests are spread across identities; a KV quota enforced per entity below the
saturation threshold keeps that entity from inducing Fill, and hard output caps take away the retained
KV that Fill needs. Skip-ahead admission, fair queuing [31], and priority scheduling [15] remove
most FCFS head-of-line blocking, though aggressive fairness can starve legitimate long-context users,
and KV held by running requests still sustains the TPOT slowdown from Squeeze. If the provider
buffers streaming output, the ITL probe loses its signal and F&S falls back to a less efficient open-loop
attack. Perplexity filters [2, 26] catch optimization-based prompts, but the plain-text prompts used
by F&S read as ordinary text and pass through. Thus, we leave a much larger scale evaluation of
F&S-type of resource consumption attack and defense to the future work.

adversarial inputs are reviewed to exclude harmful/offensive content. As the proposed method has
been timely archived by https://www.promptfoo.dev/, code artifacts consist of benchmarking
scripts and evaluation harnesses are available via the anonymous link. We would double check for
ethical issues before making them public, but not as attack tools or prompt arsenals.
Societal Impact. This work exposes a previously understudied class of system-level attack surface
in LLM serving frameworks. Understanding these attack surfaces enables service providers to
design more robust schedulers, admission controls, and per-tenant isolation mechanisms. Defense
mechanisms discussed in Appendix G provide an actionable guidance for practitioners. While this
paper proposes a new system-level attack, our ultimate goal is defensive. We hope it serves as a
wake-up call to service providers as current systems designers often treat latency degradation as a
performance issue rather than a security threat. It is essential to understand that DoS attack in LLMs
carries equivalent damage potential to conventional network DDoS attacks with asymmetric monetary
cost from the attacker and service provider, especially the operational cost and carbon emissions from
datacenters are becoming pressing issues. The second note is that this work provides a quantitative
framework for service providers to “red team” their own infrastructure as a stress test, such that they
could align attacker’s cost vs. system degradation. This would allow providers to identify essential
boundaries from a wide array of system parameters, and help providers design better admission
control policies that remain robust in a competitive and fluid environment.

B.3     vLLM Scheduler Basics

B.3.1     KV-Cache Saturation
The physical limit of GPU VRAM is a hard constraint in LLM serving. In vLLM, memory manage-
ment is orchestrated by the BlockSpaceManager2 , which maps logical KV blocks to physical GPU
memory.
To manage concurrency, the scheduler maintains a WAITING queue for pending requests and a
RUNNING queue for those currently executing on the GPU. During the autoregressive decoding
phase, the memory footprint of every active request grows monotonically. For each inference
iteration, the Scheduler assigns new physical blocks from the global free_block_queue. Unlike
computational slots that are ephemeral and reset after every step, the KV-Cache blocks allocated to
running sequences are persistent. As requests in the RUNNING queue progress through the decoding
phase, they continuously consume the remaining capacity in the free_block_queue. An adversary
can take advantage of this by exploiting long sequences to quickly deplete the memory resources and
drive the system toward a saturation point.
When this physical limit is reached, the scheduler’s admission logic creates a bottleneck. Following
a strict First-Come-First-Served (FCFS) policy, the scheduler iterates through the WAITING queue
and invokes can_allocate() for the request at the head of the line. If this check fails due to
memory exhaustion, scheduling terminates immediately to preserve request ordering and fairness,
which we term as Memory-Based HOL Blocking. This blockage creates a DoS situation even if
the computational capacity is still available, i.e., global num_batched_tokens budget used by
chunked_prefill is sufficient to accommodate subsequent, smaller requests. But due to physical
block unavailability, legitimate short requests are stalled indefinitely.

B.3.2     Preemption and Recovery Penalties
The vulnerability emerges when the system attempts to recover from this saturation. When the
cumulative memory demand of RUNNING requests surpasses the physical capacity, the scheduler
must reclaim resources to prevent a deadlock. It triggers a deterministic preemption mechanism
by invoking _preempt() to free up space. To minimize the impact on long-running tasks, vLLM
typically enforces a Last-In-First-Out (LIFO) policy and selects the most recently scheduled request
(rvictim ) to evict. For fairness, the preempted victim is not discarded but prioritized by being
prepended to the front of the WAITING queue. Depending on different implementations, vLLM
operates under one of the following principles for recovery:

        ✦ Swapping (I/O Bottleneck): Common in legacy architectures (e.g., vLLMv0), memory
          swap offloads the victim’s KV blocks from high-speed GPU VRAM to CPU host memory.
          Recovery requires migrating these blocks back over the bandwidth-constrained PCIe bus.
        ✦ Recomputation (Compute Bottleneck): In unified scheduler architectures (e.g., vLLMv1),
          the system prioritizes implementation simplicity by discarding the victim’s KV-Cache
          entirely. To resume execution, the engine must re-process the prompt and all previously
          generated tokens from scratch. This shifts the workload from the linear-time decoding back
          to the quadratic-time prefilling.

While preemption is intended to ensure stability, it exposes an asymmetry facing adversaries. The
attacker can trigger a preemption with a marginal increase in memory usage, yet the system must pay
a disproportionately high cost to recover the victim request later. By toggling across the saturation
boundary, the attacker can induce the system into back-and-forth preemption thrashing loop that
continuously imposes pressure on the memory hierarchy.

D     More Details of Fill and Squeeze Attacks

The operational logic of the Fill and Squeeze attack is formalized in Algorithm 1. In principle, it
establishes a closed-loop feedback control system that synchronizes adversarial injection with the
victim’s estimated memory state. For completeness, we illustrate the major steps in the following.
To bridge the visibility gap inherent in black-box serving, the execution cycle begins with a monitoring
phase (Lines 3–6) where low-intensity requests (pprobe ∈ PLow ) exploit the ITL side-channel to infer
the global KV-cache utilization (Ûsys ) via the estimator Mθ . This estimation allows the calculation
of a critical memory gap (∆mem = Csat − Ûsys ), representing the remaining capacity before the
scheduler forces victim eviction. Driven by this metric, the algorithm dynamically toggles its injection
strategy to manipulate the scheduler’s state machine: when the system is underutilized (∆mem >
δlarge ), the attacker enters the Fill regime, dispatching high-complexity prompts (p ∈ PHigh ) to
rapidly deplete the free_block_queue and accelerate the system toward saturation; conversely, as
the memory state approaches the fragile preemption boundary (0 < ∆mem ≤ δsmall ), the strategy
shifts to the Squeeze regime, where minimal token injections (p ∈ PLow ) are sufficient to tip the
aggregate load over the physical threshold Csat , triggering the deterministic and computationally
expensive preemption mechanism. Finally, to ensure attack sustainability, a Back-off mechanism
(Lines 14–16) creates a temporal window (twait ) when the system is overloaded, preventing the
adversary’s own requests from becoming eviction victims while trapping benign users in a resource-
draining livelock of continuous allocation and recomputation.

D.2   Effects of Chunked Prefill

Previously, we state that Continuous Batching (CB) acts as the primary logical isolation mechanism
against latency attacks on the system level. In addition to CB, modern frameworks employ a
complementary optimization known as chunked prefill to mitigate interference [1].
In traditional iteration-level scheduling, the prefill phase of a new request is atomic. If an adversary
submits a prompt with extremely long length (e.g., the prompts of Autodos exhibit this behavior [56]),
the GPU remains occupied by the prefill kernel for a significant duration, stalling the decoding
iterations of all co-located users. This creates a compute-bound HOL blocking, which is different
from the memory-based HOL blocking during the decoding phase mentioned earlier. Chunked Prefill
alleviates this by decomposing the prefill operation into smaller chunks. Instead of processing the
entire prompt in one pass, the scheduler processes a fixed budget of tokens (e.g., 512 tokens) per
iteration, interleaving these prefill chunks with the decoding steps of other active requests.
We posit that chunked prefill plays a secondary factor in mitigating latency attacks because it
addresses compute latency, ensuring that Inter-Token Latency (ITL) remains stable during the arrival
of large requests. However, it does not mitigate the memory consumption of those requests. As the
chunks are processed, the global KV-cache usage still rises monotonically. Once the memory is
exhausted, the scheduler effectively reverts to the blocking behaviors exploited by our Fill vector,
regardless of how the prefill was computed. The Squeeze stage targets the preemption logic during
the decoding phase. Since Chunked Prefill is active primarily during the initialization of a request,
it barely offers protection against the decoding phase induced by an adversary toggling the system
across the saturation boundary.

D.3   More Discussions on Model-Specific Characteristics

Our experiments reveal that the efficacy of a latency attack payload can be highly model-dependent.
An interesting case is the strong affinity of ExtendAttack for the DeepSeek-R1 model, as shown
in Tables 1 and 3. We attribute this to DeepSeek-R1’s inherent bias towards meticulous, step-by-
step reasoning and explicit self-explanation when faced with complex or unusual inputs. The core
mechanism of ExtendAttack is to force a model into a series of computationally intensive, yet


                                                      21
semantically trivial, decoding sub-tasks [59]. The poly-base ASCII obfuscation acts as a powerful
trigger for this behavior in DeepSeek-R1. The model dedicates a substantial volume of tokens
to explicitly document its reasoning process by performing base conversions, mapping values to
characters, and verifying each step, before addressing the primary query. Overall, this behavior
effectively turns the model’s own reasoning alignment into a Denial-of-Service amplifier. This
pronounced effect is observed to be unique to the DeepSeek-R1 model in our tests, suggesting a
specific architectural or fine-tuning artifact. This observation reinforces a key aspect of our work:
F&S strategy is payload-agnostic. While some payloads like ExtendAttack may have a natural
advantage on certain models, F&S provides a universal, system-level amplification mechanism.
On the cost side, on Qwen3-8B, the F&S+ExtendAttack variant not only causes the most damage
but also accomplishes with the lowest monetary cost ($0.42). This slightly counter-intuitive result
occurs because the high efficiency of the ExtendAttack payload in generating long sequences allows
the F&S to reach the “Fill” and “Squeeze” states with fewer requests, thus optimizing the attacker’s
budget. Thus, the optimal pairing of a payload with a target model family is an interesting avenue for
future research.

in PrefillAdder.add_one_req() (schedule_policy.py). Before admitting a waiting request,
the scheduler computes the remaining token budget:
  rem_total_tokens = available_size() + evictable_size() − rem_total_token_offset, (10)

where rem_total_token_offset accumulates, for every already-admitted request, the esti-

+ page_size.         A new request is rejected with AddReqResult.NO_TOKEN as soon as

This is structurally identical to the HOL-blocking mechanism in vLLM’s can_allocate() loop
(Proposition 3).
The F&S attack exploits this budget in two compounding ways. First, long-running adversarial
requests grow rem_total_token_offset rapidly, shrinking the effective budget even before physi-
cal memory is exhausted. Second, the conservative CLIP_MAX_NEW_TOKENS cap means the system
reserves 4096 future tokens per admitted request, making saturation of the budget arithmetically
easier than in vLLM’s block-counting model. Concretely, with a token pool of K tokens and n
running requests each clipped at 4096, the budget becomes negative once n · 4096 ≥ K, even if
physical memory still has free blocks.

The LPM policy does not neutralize the Fill phase. SGLang’s default scheduling policy is
Longest Prefix Match (CacheAwarePolicy.LPM), which reorders the waiting queue to maximize
RadixCache hits. However, _determine_active_policy() automatically reverts to plain FCFS
when the queue length exceeds 128 requests—precisely the high-load condition induced by the Fill
phase. Even under LPM, once add_one_req() returns NO_TOKEN for the head-of-line request, the
outer admission loop terminates and no further requests are considered. The cache-aware ordering
only affects which request sits at the head of the queue as it cannot bypass the hard budget gate.

Squeeze: retract_decode mirrors vLLM’s preemption asymmetry. When memory pres-
sure prevents new admissions, SGLang recovers headroom via retract_decode (scheduler.py),
which moves a subset of actively decoding requests back to the waiting queue. The retracted requests
lose all decode progress and must be re-prefilled from scratch (recompute mode only—SGLang has
no CPU-swap fallback at this stage), incurring an Θ(L2v ) recovery cost identical to Corollary 1. The
attacker exploits the same asymmetry: toggling the token budget across the saturation boundary at
marginal cost O(δ · price_per_token) forces repeated retract_decode cycles, each of which makes
a legitimate long-running request pay the full quadratic recomputation penalty.

To supplement the experiment validation across different serving frameworks, we validate the
effectiveness of F&S attack on SGLang across three representative target models in Table 7 and
visualize the corresponding scheduler-level dynamics in Fig. 12. Overall, F&S remains highly
efficient under SGLang’s serving architecture, inducing severe user-facing latency degradation with
low attack volume and low financial cost.
Why SGLang Could Be More Vulnerable to Latency Attacks. Corresponding to our prior
code-level analysis, from Table 7 baselines, we observe that SGLang is more vulnerable to the
latency attacks baselines. On Gemma3-12B-it, LoopLLM achieves 267.30 s vs. F&S’s 212.44 s;
on DeepSeek-R1, ExtendAttack achieves 57.53 s vs. F&S’s 55.07 s. We posit that it is due to
SGLang’s forward-looking budget model: any attack that submits requests with large expected output
lengths can rapidly saturate rem_total_tokens because each request immediately consumes up
to CLIP_MAX_NEW_TOKENS = 4096 of the reservation budget. LoopLLM and ExtendAttack, which
target very long outputs, benefit disproportionately from this mechanism on SGLang compared to
vLLM. In particular, ExtendAttack’s prefill-phase amplification (forcing extended chain-of-thought
reasoning) inflates the per-request budget reservation as well as the physical KV footprint. On
DeepSeek-R1, whose reasoning alignment generates especially long outputs under ExtendAttack’s
trigger, the baseline can match F&S’s raw TTFT.
Cost Benefits of F&S. Nevertheless, these do not diminish the contribution of F&S as the major
distinction is on the cost and request efficiency. Even when baselines achieve comparable peak TTFT,
they do so with substantially more attack traffic and higher cost. As shown in Table 7, on DeepSeek-
R1, ExtendAttack requires 180 requests at $3.08, while F&S+plain-text achieves a comparable
55.07 s TTFT with 181 requests at $2.96. On Gemma3-12B-it, LoopLLM requires 262 requests
at $0.99 to reach its peak 267 s; F&S achieves 212 s at $0.86. We can see that while brute-force
methods can sometimes match or exceed F&S in raw TTFT by aggressively saturating SGLang’s
token budget, they generally require more attack traffic or higher cost. In contrast, the Squeeze
phase forces repeated retract_decode cycles, as reflected by the oscillatory queue behavior and
repeated saturation–recovery transitions. Once retracted, actively decoding victim requests lose
decode progress and must be re-prefilled from scratch; because SGLang uses recomputation rather
than CPU-swap fallback in this path, each recovery imposes a Θ(L2v ) penalty on long-running victim
requests. The attacker, however, only pays a marginal token cost to keep the system near the admission
boundary. Thus, F&S generalizes beyond vLLM that despite implementation differences, modern

E     More Analysis of Side-Channel Probing

During inference-time attacks, the usage of KV-cache often serves as a critical latent variable that
influences both model behavior and system-level scheduling decisions. However, in black-box or
restricted-observability attack settings, an adversary typically has no direct access to the real-time
KV-cache usage.
This raises a fundamental question: Can KV-cache usage be inferred indirectly through observable
system-side signals?
Inspired by prior work on system-level side-channel analysis[35, 46, 53], we explore whether ITL
can be leveraged to probe the usage state of KV-cache. In particular, we study the correlation between
ITL and KV-cache usage and investigate the feasibility of constructing a KV-cache usage prober
based solely on time-series observations of ITL.

Analysis. The observed trend can be explained by two factors: (1) Higher concurrency leads to
greater variation in KV-cache usage, providing a wider dynamic range for correlation analysis; (2)
Under higher load, the system’s memory pressure becomes more pronounced, causing ITL to respond
more sensitively to KV-cache changes.
When KV-cache usage approaches saturation (e.g., near 100% utilization), system-level preemption,
eviction, and scheduling mechanisms are triggered more frequently. As a result, ITL may exhibit
increased non-linear behavior in extreme cases. However, in typical operating regimes (KV usage
below 90%), the linear approximation remains highly accurate.
This observation motivates the design of a temporal KV-cache usage prober that tracks ITL dynamics
over time rather than relying on instantaneous measurements, and suggests that the probe is most
effective under moderate-to-high system load conditions. In addition, these trends are consistently
observed on both Qwen3-0.6B and Qwen3-8B, suggesting that the phenomenon is not specific to a
particular model scale.

E.3   Estimator Training

The attacker trains Mθ on a surrogate system of the same model and hardware type by varying
concurrent load to sweep through different Usys levels. In a black-box API setting, the attacker can
construct such a surrogate by renting the same GPU type and serving the same model class, then
sweeping load from 1 to B concurrent requests. Thus, the ground-truth KV usage is obtained from
the surrogate’s internal metrics. Our previous results indicate high estimation accuracy.

E.5   Additional Observations and Practical Considerations

During our experiments, we observe that the coverage of KV-cache usage states in the training data
plays a critical role in KV estimation performance. If high-usage or saturated KV-cache states are
underrepresented during training, the resulting model often struggles to correctly identify such regimes
at test time. Therefore, to achieve reliable KV-cache usage inference, the training data collected
offline should span the full range of KV-cache utilization levels, ensuring sufficient representation of
extreme operating conditions.


F     System Profile

To empirically validate the memory bandwidth contention in Finding #2 and subsequent proposi-
tions, we perform a granular micro-architectural characterization of the GPU execution environment
using the NVIDIA Nsight Systems profiling suite. First, we instrument the vLLM inference loop
with NVTX markers to delineate request boundaries and capture high-fidelity CUDA kernel traces
using the command: nsys profile –output profile –trace=cuda,nvtx exp.py. Subse-
quently, to facilitate scalable and structured analysis, the raw traces are transcoded into a relational
SQLite schema via nsys export -t sqlite –output profile.sqlite profile.nsys-rep.
This approach allows for the systematic extraction of kernel-level performance metrics from the
CUPTI_ACTIVITY_KIND_KERNEL table.
Our profiling results reveal a clear divergence in scaling behavior; while compute-bound operators
(e.g., rms_norm, element-wise kernels) maintain near-constant execution durations across varying
loads, the attention mechanism (specifically the flash_fwd_splitkv_kernel) exhibits heavy-
tailed latency growth under memory pressure. These findings provide micro-architectural evidence
for the relationship, confirming that the attention operator’s sensitivity to memory bandwidth is the
primary driver of performance degradation under high context loads.


G     Discussion on Countermeasures

In this section, we provide more analysis of potential countermeasures, ranging from inherent model
constraints to active defense mechanisms. We specifically examine the behavior of Llama-3.1 under
attack and discuss the feasibility of adversarial training versus system-level guardrails.

G.1    Impact of Strict Length Constraints

Here, we provide additional results of the Fill and Squeeze attack against Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct in
Table 14. Surprisingly, the attack performance on Llama-3.1 is significantly weakened compared
to Qwen3 or Gemma3-12B-it. The TTFT under our F&S attack (126.68 ms) as well as others are
surprisingly lower than the benign baseline (144.16 ms), and the TPOT degradation is minimal. This
resilience stems from the Llama’s intrinsic configuration because Llama-3.1 enforces a maximum
context length of 8,192 tokens and tends to aggressively terminate generation earlier than its peers.
While imposing rigid constraints effectively neutralizes latency attacks by preventing the massive
KV-cache accumulation required for the Fill phase, such measures are fundamentally at odds with the
trajectory of LLM development. Specifically, as the industry aggressively pivots towards near-infinite
context windows (e.g., 1M+ tokens) to power applications like RAG and repository-level code
analysis, artificially capping generation length severely undermines service utility. Therefore, we
posit that relying on hard limits is not a sustainable long-term defense strategy.

G.2   Defensive Guardrails and Adversarial Training

Beyond inherent model constraints, we analyze active defense mechanisms. A common proposal
is the Perplexity Filter [2, 26], which detects adversarial inputs by analyzing the perplexity of
the prompt. While effective against optimization-based attacks (e.g., Engorgio) that often produce
gibberish, it fails against our plain-text prompts which are semantically coherent and natural.
Another theoretical solution is through Adversarial Training, where the model is fine-tuned on
adversarial examples to learn robustness against resource-exhaustion triggers [29] and a recent study
demonstrates success in defending against sponge examples with adversarial training [43]. However,
applying this to LLM is practically infeasible due to the prohibitive computational cost of minimax
training 70B+ parameter models and unknown risk of degrading general capabilities.
Instead of retraining, we advocate for a lightweight system-level defense inspired by efficiency
robustness studies [5]. The most viable path is implementing Behavioral Analysis that monitor
generation frequency. By tracking the ratio of output-to-input tokens per user, the system can identify
tenants who consistently trigger worst-case generation lengths. If a user’s request history exhibits a
pattern of inducing maximum output lengths at a high frequency, they can be temporarily isolated
or rate-limited [12]. This approach balances service availability with protection, addressing the
vulnerability without the need for expensive model modifications.
Other defense methods involves re-designing the scheduler but requires a more thorough validation.
For example, we could allow the scheduler to skip the HOL request if it cannot allocate blocks and
admit smaller subsequent request more flexibly. This strategy directly breaks the memory-based HOL
blocking but may potentially violate FCFS processing order, and adaptive attackers could also exploit
Plow to occupy these smaller subsequent requests. On the other hand, the LIFO victim selection
policy could be updated with a largest-memory-consumer policy, which is akin to behavioral analysis
that make the attacker’s own high-memory requests more likely to be preempted. However, it may
increase the recomputation overhead for benign long-context requests. We can see that there is indeed
an unavoidable trade-off between performance and robustness. We defer a holistic design and a
thorough quantitative evaluation to the future work.

G.3   vLLM Configuration and Attack-Mechanism Mapping

Rather than sweep every combination of vLLM parameters, we summarize how each knob moves a
specific part of the F&S vulnerability surface. This complements the empirical TP2/TP4/TP8 sweep
in Fig. 8(a), which already validates the predicted capacity trend.
The parameters that determine admission capacity are max_num_seqs, gpu_memory_utilization,
and the tensor-parallel degree. max_num_seqs sets the effective batch size, so it directly controls
concurrent admission and aggregate KV demand; larger values raise the steady-state KV pressure and
shorten the time-to-saturation for the Fill phase. gpu_memory_utilization together with tensor
parallelism determines the total KV-cache capacity, and thus the Fill saturation threshold Csat : higher
capacity forces the attacker to pay more before reaching Csat , and at TP8 the fixed-strength attack we
evaluate is neutralized.
The remaining knobs primarily move the cost profile of a Squeeze cycle rather than its feasi-
bility. block_size controls allocation granularity near saturation; smaller blocks reduce over-
shoot around Csat and make the Squeeze-triggering margin δ finer-grained. The preemption mode
(recomputation vs. swap) and the associated swap_space determine the per-preemption recovery
cost analyzed in Corollary 1: recomputation makes each Squeeze event pay a Θ(L2v ) prefill penalty,

while swap turns it into a PCIe-bandwidth-bound Ω(Lv /BWPCIe ) transfer. Finally, the scheduling

Taken together, these trends explain why over-provisioning (more capacity) and admission redesign
(skip-ahead, fair queuing) are the two most impactful defense levers, whereas block-size and
preemption-mode adjustments primarily change the cost profile of an attack rather than its fea-
sibility. We do not report a full sensitivity sweep across every parameter combination and encourage
operators to red-team their specific deployments using this mapping.

H    Limitations
We list here the concrete scope boundaries of F&S and of the evaluation, complementing the “Scope
and Boundary of Claims” paragraph in the main-text threat model (Section 5.1).
Evaluation scope. The main attack experiments—Table 1, all ablations, the real-world trace study,
and the TP2/TP4/TP8 defense sweep—run on a single 8×L40S node. Multi-node evidence is limited
to the passive prober observation on a two-replica production deployment reported in Appendix E.4;
we did not launch active F&S attacks against production traffic. Pipeline parallelism, speculative
decoding, and tensor parallelism split across nodes have not been validated, as those deployments can
change token cadence, KV placement, and scheduler state in ways that our results do not attempt to
characterize. Our baseline comparison is likewise with respect to the published fixed-schedule attacks
(Engorgio, LoopLLM, ExtendAttack): a generic latency-feedback wrapper around each baseline is a
different research question, and the component ablation in Appendix D.1 only shows that adaptive
dispatch alone is insufficient to reproduce F&S’s effect, not that every possible adaptive redesign has
been surveyed.
Estimator and side channel. The LightGBM estimator is trained on a matching surrogate of the same
model class and GPU type. Throughout the paper, “black-box” refers to the absence of victim-side
access; it does not mean that a single frozen predictor transfers zero-shot across arbitrary hardware,
and Table 12 quantifies the graceful degradation observed when the GPU, model, and network path
change simultaneously. The closed loop also depends on visible streaming per-token timestamps: if a
provider hides token-level timing through server-side buffering, or injects sufficiently large emission
jitter, F&S degrades to a less efficient open-loop injection. We characterize this qualitatively in
Section 6 but do not sweep a jitter-versus-degradation curve.
Framework and theoretical scope. Results are reported on vLLM 0.11.2 and SGLang commit
99c0b62. Future releases may patch the specific can_allocate() and add_one_req() paths we
analyse, and although the underlying Fill/Squeeze taxonomy should remain applicable, the numerical
thresholds will shift. Finally, Propositions 1–3 and Corollary 1 identify sufficient conditions under
which CB logical isolation, ITL–KV correlation, FCFS-HOL blocking, and preemption asymmetry
emerge; they are explanatory models rather than universal queuing-theoretic guarantees, and their
applicability depends on the deployment assumptions in Section 5.1.
